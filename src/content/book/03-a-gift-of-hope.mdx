The frozen breeze was colder than the usual winds. A blizzard had come with the southern currents and it only got worse with time. Through the snow two figures appeared. They were barely visible, dragging a wheelbarrow with great effort but minimal results. Walking became harder as the snow started to pile. It already covered them below the knees and the heavy thing they carried started to get stuck.

They have been traveling for as long as their feet allowed them, then they slept and walked some more. The very few people that survived the unlit days did so; always moving in small groups. The lack of Sun didn’t only affect their bodies making them pale and weak, it also messed up their minds. It was common for lone travelers to end up gone in the head, the twisted world had got to them. The few survivors of the initial darkening died slowly until only a bunch of them were left. It eventually took the life of kings and beggars alike; hunger does not discern. Only the smart survived, wanderers who permanently moved forward, not always knowing where to. But those who had no direction perished first, they couldn’t find sustenance and died. Those who survived were the ones who knew how to avoid their demise. They had to be efficient about it, moving when it was wise and settling in when they found shelter. <span class="fx-key">They had no room for mistakes for Death was the only certainty they had.</span> That was the case for the two figures on the white snow. They knew perfectly their destination and they had just set foot on it.

This place was once called Oldraan, commonly known as the Empire of Sun, now just as cold as anywhere—if not even colder. They had reached it a few months ago after crossing the dead seas.

The man who was pushing the cart was of robust silhouette but weak in appearance. His face—covered in snow—looked old and constantly exhausted. He had a big beard that merged with his long brown hair, of course being useful to isolate the freezing weather. From his neck hung a golden locket being this the only thing he carried that didn’t seem to feel the pass of time. He wore enough layers of old animal fur on him to keep him warm—but most importantly to maintain his feet dry.

“We’re close, my dear,” he said. He had a big smile on him.

He had to speak loudly or his words would be lost in the wind, but the smile on his face was there the entire time. The wind became meaner and blew harder on their backs. It was difficult to call that place a home; it was just as barren and dead as anywhere else they had been before. In any case, it treated them even worse since it greeted them with a not so welcoming storm.

The man looked at the map again and started to cough, he had to stop for a second and recover his breath.

“Are you alright?” the girl with him asked.

“I’m perfect, my dear. I've never felt stronger.”

“Are you sure? You sound sick. You need to get warmer,” she said with concern.

He nodded. The old man had given her his coat and she felt guilty to let him freeze in that weather.

“I’m old, but I can take some bad weather,” he shouted against the current of air still holding his smile. “I’m fine. Why would I lie to you?”

Holding the map was extremely difficult and the snow covered most of it, but he was certain that they had arrived to the old Empire of Oldraan. They were going north—towards the pouring Sun—and the wind seemed to move towards the same direction, so he tried to think for an alternative path. He saw a forest not too far from where they stood, it also corresponded with the map. For the lack of any other option, he decided to go there.

“We can set our camp in there,” added the old man pointing at the trees in the distance. “We’ll need to wait until the storm is gone.”

*Whatever is best for you*, he thought.

The daughter nodded. She looked very much alike his father, both pale of skin for the lack of Sun but still darkened by their way of living. She was shorter in size but stronger in spirit with her youth well hidden by the tiredness. Her hair—longer than her father’s—was of a reddish color, almost unnoticeable for all the sludge she had on herself. Her deep blue eyes contrasted with the reds of her head. <span class="fx-key">She was the fire that kept the old man going.</span> Elana was her name—a name she shared with her deceased mother.

With the current of air growing desperately, they dragged their belongings with a lot of struggle. Since their vision was restricted to a few meters in front, had they not had their compass on them, they could had gotten lost and buried by the snow. The fatigue already set on their tired bones when they finally reached the beginning of the forest.

The girl sighed with relief. The father had told her all her life about the Empire and her eagerness to witness it could be seen in the twinkling of her eyes. He smiled. He never thought it would take them so long to reach it, but they were finally there.

*Seven years*, he thought. *But this will be the best for her. <span class="fx-key">This is the only thing I can give her. This is hope.</span>*

<MoodShift theme="dusk" label="THE BACKWOODS FOREST" />

They entered the forest with caution. The trees were so close together that snow didn’t pierce through them, the sky was barely visible. Tall, black trees rising to the sky, with their wood dry and dead. Some trunks were like coal, and still useful for fires, but others had hardened becoming impossible to break. They had burned with the fires of the skies. From the bottom of it you could not see them end, and it <span class="fx-stretch">extended for miles in all directions,</span> getting darker than night the further they got into it.

The father tried to become conscious of his surroundings, he sharpened his hearing, for any incoming threat that could be creeping in the dark. He was always preoccupied in situations like this, but Elana never learned the fear of dark since her only worries were of hunger and coldness. He made sure of that, to keep her safe.

They set their camp for the night when they reached a clearing in the woods. The father knew that even when the trees were protecting them from the storm, the night in a forest could be fatal for its freezing temperatures. He first took some of the wood nearby and made a fire big enough to keep them warm. While he recollected sticks and branches, the daughter started to look for water. She brought a shovel with her. On those dry days they had to extract the dirty water of the abundant mud that usually covered the soils, filtering it with old cloths and rocks and boiling it up afterwards. The process was slow and tedious—and just a small amount of water came from it—they had to do this very often. Thankfully the snow provided them with cleaner water than what they normally drank but it still had to be melted first. Eating ice or snow, while not harmful by itself, could drop the body temperature below the average levels. Besides the fire she set the snow to melt, a pile of it over a pipe that led to a water bottle. And the water started dripping, making a sound when it hit the bottom.

Once the fire was lit and the water was boiling, the father took out a small sack from the bag he carried. It was filled with some grains and he proceeded to make them into a soup, only seasoned with a pinch of salt, resulting in a tasteless meal like most of the food they’ve had. The stew shined brightly with the colors of the fire. They sat, finally, and tried to warm their hands before eating. They ate in silence.

The way they got their food was a process even harder, they had to search for an old crop field and shovel through the layers of dirt that had covered them, they would reach a thick layer of ice. In that layer—if they were lucky enough—they could find food that was at least barely edible.

The bowls were already empty. The old man put away their dishes to clean them later. He took a map from his pocket and spread it through the trunk of a tree, with his compass at a side.

“We have reached the backwoods forest, just at the verge of the kingdom. If we continue through it, we’ll arrive at some outland town. We can rest in there for a few days.” The father stopped to cough; he usually did when he talked too much. “We’re not far away.”

“Do you think that we’ll make it?” asked Elana visibly preoccupied by the state of her father.

He nodded with a smile. “We have come a long way; we’ll arrive at the city of gods in a few weeks. I can be sure of that.”

The father squeezed his necklace, and broke in a cough more violent than the last one. On his hand he could see <span class="fx-key">some blood that he hid behind his back.</span> His health was deteriorating rapidly but he did his best to appear normal. Elana wasn’t a fool and he suspected that she knew he was hiding something from her.

The old man continued to expand about the forgotten lands. Elana had heard a lot about the city of gods from his father, the same stories his grandparent told him and who knows who told them. This place was as old as the memory of those who transmitted its tales. The last fallen Empire.

<MoodShift theme="void" label="NIGHT IN THE WOODS" />

When Elana thought that her father was sleeping, she stood up and strolled around. She liked to do so after dusk. The nights were cold and dark, but they let her feel protected. She knew that nothing hid on the shadows; she had nothing to fear.

<span class="fx-impact">Sticks cracked in the distance.</span> A tall shape stood behind the woods. Was her sight betraying her? She rubbed her eyes in stupefaction. <span class="fx-ghost">The shadow was gone.</span>

*The stories of my father must be getting in my head*, she thought and dismissed it as just pareidolia—a delusion of her senses.

She walked far away from the camp, into a place where she would not be heard or bothered. She used to imitate the sounds of animals that her father taught her—looking in the woods waiting to find one. Her dream was to see a bird. A live one, not like the pictures in the books. She sang like she thought the birds did, trying to call them. She never found one, however, she never had seen another soul other than his father. They were alone.

Except for that night. She felt something watching her, closely. There was no wind, yet she felt the trees move in the background. She felt uncomfortable, but her logic told her that she was being paranoid.

*Just the wind*, Elana thought. *Or—maybe a bird.*

She smiled. <span class="fx-ghost">The presence she felt was gone,</span> the night was hers again. It wrapped around her and reassured that she was secure.

Sometimes she liked looking at the <span class="fx-stretch">blank sky</span> and imagine the stars, like a hundred fires burning over her, then she would draw the constellations she imagined on the ground. She liked to draw, she saw that sometimes words were not enough for her, so she drew constantly. Every camp they set was left with a unique mark. That way, she thought, if someone was still out there, they could follow the path of marks. She also had a notebook on her at all times, she used it to sketch images that impressed her. She thought that she was good at it.

*Someday*, she thought. <span class="fx-key">*I will find a bird and I will draw it.*</span>

<span class="fx-whisper">Some day.</span>

Elana went back to the camp and laid beside her father to maintain some of their body heat. Elana slept fast, she was very light of sleep and the fatigue made it easier. Besides her, the old man was still awake. He knew how his daughter liked her night walks but never commented on it, he didn’t want to take away from the freedom she felt. He faked being asleep every night. He was relieved when he heard her sleeping, after all, he was trying to gag his continuous cough with a cloth. Soon it got tainted with blood and he took some more. He coughed what he couldn’t during the whole day, holding his necklace fiercely. This was the time when he didn’t contain himself, only making sure to not make a sound.

The cloths full of <span class="fx-sharp">blood</span> burned with the sticks and woods. The cracking fire, the dripping water and the wind breaking on the branches snuggled them up. And the night went on quietly.

*Only a bit more*, he thought. *I only need to push for a bit.*

The father couldn’t sleep much, he never did. He used to think and wonder about her daughter until his body could no more and passed out from exhaustion. He tried to hide his illness from her daughter since he knew that soon she would be traveling alone.

<span class="fx-whisper">“The only thing I can give her,”</span> he murmured.
